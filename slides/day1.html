Before you write

Name the reader and their decision before you pick a document

Who reads?

One person, by role: the operations director, a new developer, the head of reception. "Everyone" is nobody.

What will they do?

Install, approve, choose, repeat, rely. The verb decides the document and its first line.

What do they know?

You know the project too well to see its gaps: write for what the reader already knows, not for you.

What goes wrong. A document written for "the stakeholders" answers nobody's question and buries the one decision someone has to make.

Ask the room to name the last document they wrote and its one reader. Most will name two or three. The third card is what Google's Technical Writing One course calls the curse of knowledge: the writer cannot unsee what they know. Sources: developers.google.com/tech-writing/one.

As sent

Uncertainty in plain words

Say how sure you are, and on how many cases

Says nothing

"Method B is significantly better at catching run-outs."

Says how sure

"Method B flagged 52 of 60 past run-outs (0.87). With 60 cases, the 95% interval runs from 0.76 to 0.93."

  • The count tells the reader how much evidence there is; the interval tells them how far to trust the rate.
  • On nine cases, 7 of 9 (0.78) has an interval from 0.45 to 0.94: say so, or do not report the rate.

Figures from labs/_tools/slide_examples.py, using the Wilson interval that the tagger's own code computes. How to compute an interval belongs to FND-AIC-103; this course is about writing it so a non-specialist reads it right. "Significantly" means "statistically tested" to some readers and "a lot" to others: avoid it unless you mean the first and say which test.

Hedges

Replace a hedge with a number, a condition or "not measured"

HedgeReplace with
"quite good", "generally fine"the rate and its count: 41 of 60
"seems better"the difference, and whether the intervals overlap
"should be enough"the condition: enough if at least 20 new cases are urgent
"no issues"what was checked: 60 weeks, no missing values
"probably fine for other branches""not measured on other branches"
"Not measured" is a finding. It tells the reader where the evidence stops.

Ask the room for the hedge they use most. The lab's checker flags a list of hedges in any sentence that has no number, as a style linter would; Vale is a real tool that does this with rules written in YAML (docs.vale.sh). The checker is a prompt to look again, not a verdict: a hedge beside a number can be honest.

A README's job

A stranger installs, runs and checks the project without asking anyone

Install

Every command as typed, from which folder, with the versions it needs and any file to create.

Run

The one command that does the project's job on supplied data, and where the output goes.

Check

The tests, and the exact line they print when all is well: "it ran" is not "it works".

Then the rest. What it is for and not for, where things are, its limits and its owner.

Ask: what is the first thing you look for in a repository you have never seen? Usually "how do I run it". A README that opens with the project's history fails the bottom-line test of session 1. The owner line matters: a stranger needs to know whom to tell when it breaks.

What the writer forgets

A stranger stops at the steps the writer does without thinking

  • The language version the code needs, and how to get it.
  • A file to create: a settings file copied from an example, an empty folder.
  • The folder a command runs from.
  • The real command, when an old note names a script that no longer exists.
  • What success looks like: the line a working run prints.
What goes wrong. The writer tests the README on their own laptop, where every one of these is already true.

These are the usual gaps of any small project; the urgency tagger has several of them, and lab 1.2 finds which. Do not tell the room which ones the tagger has: the first step of the lab is to try it and list what you had to guess.


```text

```
The text block is a promise. A reader, or a test, compares it with what the screen shows.

The pharmacy forecast is a parallel example; it is not in the course files. Point at the two fenced blocks: sh for what you type, text for what you should see. The lab's clean-run tool reads exactly these two kinds of block: it runs the first and looks for the second's lines in the output.

Not everything in one file

A README points to the other kinds of documentation

Tutorial

Learning by doing, step by step.

How-to guide

One task, for someone who knows the basics.

Reference

Every option and field, to look up.

Explanation

Why it works the way it does.

The README is a front door: install, run, check, then links to the rest.

These four forms are the Diátaxis framework (diataxis.fr). A small project rarely needs all four; the point is not to cram a tutorial, a reference and an explanation into the README. For the tagger the design document is the explanation and the CLI help is the reference.

Design document

A design document is written before the code, for the person who approves it

  • Summary: the change and the decision asked for.
  • Problem and goals, and what is out of scope.
  • The design, with a diagram of what flows where.
  • Alternatives and why not.
  • Risks and failure cases, with what catches each.
  • Open questions, reviewers and the approver.

Its test

Could the approver say no for a good reason after reading it? If every alternative is a straw man, they cannot.

The approver is not the author and not the reviewer: name three roles. A design document that has no alternatives is a decision already taken and dressed as a question. The participants outline one in lab 1.1 (scenario 2) and may write one in the capstone if their project needs it.

The decision memo

One decision, one page, one signature

1
decision, asked as a question
450
words at most: one page
3
roles named: author, reviewer, decider
The reader has ten minutes. Everything that does not help them choose goes in an appendix or the experiment report.

The 450-word page is the course's own rule, checked by the lab's memo checker. Three roles: the person who writes it is not the person who reviews it, and neither signs it. That separation is what makes the signature mean something.

Five parts

Bottom line, options, trade-offs, recommendation, what would change it

Bottom linethe recommendation and the request
→
Optionseach one, named once
→
Trade-offsmeasured, with intervals
→
Recommendationargued from the reader's needs
→
What would change itconditions with numbers

Press once per part. The last part is the one most memos lack. It turns a recommendation into something the reader can monitor: if the volume, the price or the measured quality moves past a line, the decision comes back. The lab's rubric scores these five parts, two points each.

Options

Keep the current way as an option, and name every option the same way throughout

  • The status quo is a real option with real costs: say what staying costs.
  • Same names, same order in the options, the table and the recommendation.
  • No straw men: an option nobody would pick wastes the reader's ten minutes.
  • Unmeasured options go in "what would change this", not in the table.
What goes wrong. "Method B", then "the new model", then "the ML option": the reader wonders if they are three things.

Link back to the glossary slide: option names are terms too. An unmeasured idea ("what if we combined them?") is tempting to add; put it where it belongs, as a condition or a next step, so the table holds only measured options.

Put the options side by side on the measures the reader cares about

A parallel example, synthetic, from labs/_tools/slide_examples.py.

Not lab data: the lab compares three urgency taggers on quality, cost and latency. Point at the interval column first: the reader's question is not only "which is higher" but "how sure". Ask: if the pharmacy needs at least 0.80 of run-outs flagged, what can we say about Method B? Its point estimate meets it, its interval reaches down to 0.76.

Recommendation

Argue from the reader's requirement, not from "the best score"

From the score

"Method B is better on every quality measure, so we recommend it."

From the requirement

"The pharmacy needs 0.80 of run-outs flagged within SAR 20 a month. Only Method B is likely to meet it, and it costs SAR 15.17."

Requirements first. State each one with its number, then show which options meet it and how surely.

A score without a requirement invites the reader to ask "is that good enough?". The requirement comes from the reader's side (here a pharmacy lead; in the lab, the head of reception) and the memo should quote it. "Likely" is earned by the interval: say what it rests on.

What would change this

Name the lines that, once crossed, bring the decision back

  • A measured threshold: if a larger test puts Method B below 0.80, bring a new option.
  • A volume: at SAR 3.50 a run, about six runs a month fit the SAR 20 budget; daily runs do not.
  • A price: at SAR 4.62 a run, weekly runs cost SAR 20.02 a month and break it.
Show the working. Each computed number gets one line: 20 / 3.50 = 5.71 runs.

Figures from labs/_tools/slide_examples.py. A condition with a number can be checked next quarter by someone who did not write the memo. The memo checker accepts a computed number only if it is in the evidence or shown on a line that starts with "Working:".

Every claim checked

Every number in the memo traces to the evidence or to its working

  • Copy numbers from the evidence; never retype them from memory.
  • Round once, the same way everywhere.
  • A number you computed has its working beside it.
  • The reviewer checks numbers before style.

Checkpoint

Which sentence belongs in the pharmacy memo?

The pharmacy needs at least 0.80 of run-outs flagged. Method B flagged 52 of 60, 0.87, with a 95% interval from 0.76 to 0.93.

"Method B meets the 0.80 requirement."
"Method B cannot be shown to meet 0.80, so we cannot recommend it."
"Method B is significantly better than the current method."
"Method B likely meets 0.80; on 60 cases its interval reaches down to 0.76."
Why. It states the point, the doubt and what the doubt rests on, so the reader can decide with open eyes.

Answer: D. Many pick A because the point estimate clears the line. Ask what they would say if a larger test came back at 0.78. B is the opposite error and would make every small-sample decision impossible.
